## 1. Insurance

### 1.1 Who regulates insurance in Zambia

Insurance in Zambia is supervised by the **Pensions and Insurance Authority (PIA)**, a statutory body established under the *Pension Scheme Regulation Act No. 28 of 1996* and the *Insurance Act No. 27 of 1997*. NAPSA (pensions) and PIA (insurance) sit under the same authority; the Bank of Zambia supervises banks and the Securities Exchange Commission supervises capital markets.

Source: https://www.pia.org.zm/ (checked 2 November 2026)

**If an insurer or broker treats you badly, this is the escalation path:** complain to the insurer first; if unresolved, escalate to PIA. PIA states it acknowledges complaints within 3 days and investigates within **10 business days** under Regulation 19.

Source: https://www.pia.org.zm/ (complaints procedure) and https://pia.ontech.co.zm/ (10-business-day / 3-day acknowledgement SLA, cited 2 November 2026)

> **Lesson hook:** Most Zambians who "lose" money to an insurer do not lose it to fraud — they lose it because they never complained, or complained to the wrong place, or let the 6-month claim window expire. PIA's own public notice for 2024 listed 23 licensed general insurers and 10 licensed long-term insurers; only a small fraction of the population holds a policy.
>
> Source: PIA public notice, "Licensed Insurance Entities" (2024 list) via https://www.scribd.com/document/792367249/Licensed-Insurance-Entities-January-2024 (checked 2 November 2026 — see "Not verified" for the current 2026 list)

### 1.2 The four kinds of cover a household should understand

| Type of cover | What it actually pays for | Who sells it in Zambia | Key trap |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Funeral policy** | A cash lump sum on death to pay for the burial; some products also deliver a coffin, tent, transport, grocery cheque | Underwritten by a life insurer (African Life, Prudential, Sanlam, ZSIC, Hollard, Liberty, Madison, ABSA, One Life, Best Life) and sold via banks, funeral homes, burial societies | Waiting periods of 3–6 months. If someone dies inside that window, **nothing is paid** |
| **Life policy (term / whole life)** | A lump sum to the named beneficiary on death of the insured life | Same long-term insurers, mostly through banks and brokers | Premiums are paid for years; if you stop, cover stops |
| **Education / school-fees policy** | An endowment that pays school fees in instalments at Primary, Secondary or Tertiary stage | ZSIC Life, Madison Life, Prudential, Zanaco/Prudential, Hollard | Zambia abolished public-school fees in 2022, so this is mainly for **private, boarding and tertiary** fees |
| **Health (medical) insurance** | Pays for care at accredited facilities | **NHIMA** (compulsory, state) is the main one; private medical schemes exist for higher-income households | NHIMA has a 4-month waiting period and does not cover everything |

### 1.3 How funeral policies work in Zambia (the part that matters most)

A funeral policy is simple in concept: you pay a small fixed premium every month; when an insured person dies, the insurer pays the benefit out to the nominated beneficiary so the family does not have to sell assets or borrow in the middle of a bereavement.

**How it is normally structured:**

1. **You pick a "sum assured"** (the amount paid on death) and pay a monthly premium. The premium is usually an *amount per person per month*, and larger sums assured cost more.
2. **Many people are covered on one policy** — main life, spouse, children, and often parents/parents-in-law or extended family, up to a set number of lives.
3. **No medical exam.** Funeral cover in Zambia is deliberately designed to be bought without medical tests or medical evidence.
4. **There is a waiting period.** If the insured dies during the waiting period, the policy does not pay.
5. **A claim is a document exercise**, not an automatic payment.

**Typical cost and cover — examples currently marketed in Zambia:**

| Product | Cover | Premium | Waiting period | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Zanaco Flexi Farewell** (underwritten by Prudential Life) | K20,000 – K100,000 | Not published on the page | 6 months for death from natural causes | Up to 18 people on one policy (main life, 1 spouse, 6 children, 10 extended family); policy never lapses after the waiting period if premiums are paid |
| **FSG / Ideal Funeral Home group funeral plans** (underwritten by African Life) | Group benefits, not a cash sum | Not published; once-off joining fee **K20 per nuclear family, K20 per single member, K10 per additional member** | 3 months (waived for accidental death); **24 months for suicide** | Group plans need a minimum of 20 people; benefits include grocery cheque, coffin, undertaker, programme, mortuary, tent and chairs, grave cover, transport; schemes are **non-refundable** |
| **Stanbic Ubuntu Family Life Plan** | Not stated as a cash figure on the page | Not published | Not published on the page | **No medical tests**; payout within 48 hours; covers spouse, children, up to 4 parents/parents-in-law and up to 6 dependants below 75 |

| **Old Mutual / Best Life funeral plans** | Cash payout | Not published on the site pages checked | Not published | Claims submitted by dialling **\*120\*6672**; Old Mutual aims to pay **final-expense death benefits within 48 hours** and all other death claims within **15 working days** |

### 1.4 What a funeral claim actually requires

This is the single most valuable, concrete table in this topic: the difference between a claim that pays and a claim that is rejected is almost always paperwork.

**Documents needed for a funeral/life death claim (common across Zambian insurers):**

| Document | Notes |
|---|---|
| Fully completed claim form | Signed by the claimant; if the beneficiary cannot sign, a **fingerprint certified by a Commissioner of Oaths at a police station** is accepted |
| Death certificate | Some insurers accept a burial order or medical certificate of cause of death instead |
| Certified copy of NRC / national ID of the deceased | Passport or driver's licence also accepted by some products |
| Certified copy of birth certificate | For a child who died |
| Certified copy of the claimant's ID | |
| Proof of relationship | Marriage certificate / birth certificate, if the deceased is not the policyholder |
| Police report | Required only where death was accidental |
| Policy document | FSG group funeral plans require the policy document |

Sources (checked 2 November 2026):
- FNB Family Protection Plan claims list: https://www.fnbzambia.co.zm/insurance/familyProtection.html and T&Cs PDF https://www.fnbzambia.co.zm/downloads/zambia/insurance/FNB_FamilyProtectionPlan_Ts&Cs.pdf
- Stanbic Ubuntu Family Life Plan claims list (accepts death certificate **or** burial order **or** police report for accident **or** medical certificate of cause of death): https://www.stanbicbank.co.zm/zambia/personal/products-and-services/insure-what-matters/ubuntu-family-life-plan
- FSG / Ideal Funeral Home claims list (policy document, authorisation letter if beneficiary is not the principal, NRC of deceased, NRC of principal/beneficiary, death certificate or notification): https://fsg.co.zm/insurance

**Two time limits that catch people out (both from the FNB/Sanlam terms):**

- The **claim must be submitted within 6 months** of the date of death of the insured — the insurer reserves the right to reject a late claim.
- All supporting **documentation must be submitted within 12 months** of the date of death.

Source: FNB Family Protection Plan terms & conditions, https://www.fnbzambia.co.zm/downloads/zambia/insurance/FNB_FamilyProtectionPlan_Ts&Cs.pdf (checked 2 November 2026)

**Who gets paid if no beneficiary was nominated:** the spouse; failing that, the closest qualifying relative who is an insured person; failing that, the deceased's estate.

Source: FNB Family Protection Plan terms & conditions, same URL (checked 2 November 2026)

### 1.5 Education (school-fees) policies — and why they matter less than they used to in Zambia

**The policy context changed in 2022.** The Government announced the "Education for All" policy on 29 October 2021, operationalised by a circular on 8 December 2021, abolishing **all formal and informal fees** in general education — pre-primary, primary, lower and upper secondary — including tuition, Grade 12 examination fees and Parent Teacher Association (PTA) fees. School grants to schools were increased six-fold, from **K186 million in 2021 to K1.2 billion in 2022 and K1.4 billion in 2023**.

Sources (checked 2 November 2026):
- World Bank, Zambia education financing analysis: https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/server/api/core/bitstreams/57b013c6-df8b-4c8b-98a4-981afdfe7179/content
- UNICEF Zambia Budget Brief — Education 2023: https://www.unicef.org/esa/media/12566/file/UNICEF-Zambia-Budget-Brief-Education-2023.pdf

**What this means for a lesson:** an education policy is now mainly about **boarding fees, private schools, and tertiary education** — not about paying basic school fees in a government school. Members should be told this plainly, otherwise they will buy a product they do not need.

**Examples currently on the market:**

| Product | Minimum cover / sum assured | Structure |
|---|---|---|
| **ZSIC Life School Fees Plan** | Minimum sum assured **K1,000,000**, subject to a minimum premium of **K35,000** (inclusive of a K5,000 policy fee) | Fees payable for **7 years** (Primary), **5 years** (Secondary), **4 years** (Tertiary); premium term runs from the child's current age to the stage of entry |
| **ZSIC Life Education Policy** | Not published | Endowment covering Primary, Secondary or Tertiary; flexible terms **5–20 years**; 100% guaranteed payout |
| **Zanaco Smart Child Education Plan** (underwritten by Prudential) | Not published | Minimum term 5 years, maximum 20; child age 1–16; **1% maturity bonus for each year in force** on terms of 7 years and above; **5% of the maturity benefit paid as a funeral benefit** if the policyholder dies; premiums waived on the policyholder's death, permanent disability or critical illness; **policy lapses if premiums are not paid for 3 consecutive months**; reinstatement within 12 months on payment of all outstanding premiums |

### 1.6 National Health Insurance (NHIMA) — compulsory health cover for civil servants

**NHIMA is not optional.** The National Health Insurance Scheme is established under the *National Health Insurance Act No. 2 of 2018*, managed by the **National Health Insurance Management Authority (NHIMA)**, and described by NHIMA itself as *"The compulsory National Health Insurance scheme."*

Sources (checked 2 November 2026):
- NHIMA official site: https://www.nhima.co.zm/
- The National Health Insurance Act No. 2 of 2018 (Parliament of Zambia): https://www.parliament.gov.zm/sites/default/files/documents/acts/The%20National%20Health%20Insurance%20Actl,%20No.2%20of%202018%20Sig.pdf

**Who qualifies:** all citizens and established residents aged 18 and above must register. Registration is compulsory for both formal and informal sector workers.

Source: PaySpace Zambia Payroll Tax Guide 2026, https://www.payspace.com/media/ZM-PAYROLL-SOLUTION-TAX-GUIDE-SUMMARY-2026.pdf (checked 2 November 2026) — secondary source; the Act itself confirms the scheme is for "all citizens and established residents"

**What it costs:**

| Person | Contribution | Base | Frequency |
|---|---|---|---|
| Employee | **1%** | Basic salary | Monthly, deducted by employer |
| Employer | **1%** | Basic salary | Monthly, remitted with the employee's share |
| Self-employed / informal sector | 1% of declared income (formal schedule) | Declared income | Monthly |
| **Informal sector, practical rate** | **About K50 per household per month**, covering up to 7 members (one principal + six family members) | Flat household rate | Monthly |

Sources (checked 2 November 2026):
- Contribution rates 1% employee / 1% employer / 1% self-employed: NHIMA actuarial report (2022), Schedule III table, https://p4h.world/app/uploads/2024/06/2022-Final-NHIMA-Actuarial-Report-2022-2032-1.x73677.pdf (also reproduced in https://zamcalc.com/blog/how-nhima-works-zambia — secondary) — the underlying authority is *National Health Insurance (General) Regulations, 2019, SI No. 63 of 2019*, Third Schedule
- Informal-sector K50 per household per month, up to 7 members, and the 2% total for the formal sector: Ministerial Statement by Mrs Masebo to the National Assembly, 10 July 2024, Parliament of Zambia: https://www.parliament.gov.zm/sites/default/files/images/publication_docs/Ministerial%20Statement%20%20by%20Mrs%20Masebo%20-%2010.07.24.pdf
- Employer must remit employee + employer contribution within 10 days of the end of the calendar month to which the earnings relate: PaySpace Zambia Payroll Tax Guide 2026, https://www.payspace.com/media/ZM-PAYROLL-SOLUTION-TAX-GUIDE-SUMMARY-2026.pdf

**Important for the PAYE lesson (a common misconception):** the NHIMA 1% is charged on **basic salary**, not gross pay. Allowances are outside the base. Unlike NAPSA, **NHIMA has no earnings ceiling**.

Source: https://zamcalc.com/blog/how-nhima-works-zambia (checked 2 November 2026 — secondary source, but the 1%-of-basic-salary base traces to SI 63 of 2019, Third Schedule)

**Who does NOT pay (but must still register):**

- Persons aged **65 and above**
- Persons declared **poor and vulnerable** by the Ministry responsible for social welfare
- Persons **mentally or physically disabled** and unable to work

Sources (checked 2 November 2026): *National Health Insurance Act No. 2 of 2018*, section 16 (https://www.parliament.gov.zm/sites/default/files/documents/acts/The%20National%20Health%20Insurance%20Actl,%20No.2%20of%202018%20Sig.pdf); NHIMA FAQ and membership welcome letter, https://www.nhima.co.zm/elementor-1783/ and https://www.studocu.com/row/document/university-of-zambia/secondary-teachers-computer-science-diploma/24192617-impressive/100971845

**What it covers (the benefit package):**

| Covered | Notes |
|---|---|
| Out-patient registration and consultation | Including fast-track OPD |
| In-patient medical care | Ward, ICU, HDU, nursing, oxygen, medicines, consumables — at agreed daily tariff rates |
| Diagnostics | Laboratory and radiology: X-ray, CT, MRI, ultrasound, mammogram |
| Major and minor surgery | Including orthopaedic, ENT, anaesthetics, implants, disposables |
| Maternity, neonatal and paediatric care | Normal and caesarean delivery, antenatal and postnatal care |
| Pharmaceuticals, medical consumables and blood products | Per the National Essential Medicines List |
| Physiotherapy and rehabilitation | In-patient and out-patient |
| Dental and oral health | Fillings, gum treatment, extractions, braces, dentures, root canal |
| Vision care and spectacles | Spectacles **once every three years**; cataract, glaucoma, trauma care |
| Mental health services, cancer/oncology services, medical appliances and prostheses | Listed as covered services |

Source: NHIMA Benefit Package, https://nhima.co.zm/benefit-package/ (checked 2 November 2026)

**How a member actually uses it:** go to an NHIMA-accredited facility (public, mission or private), present the NHIMA card or number at reception, and the facility bills NHIMA directly — no cash payment for covered services. NHIMA has accredited **more than 350 facilities** country-wide, and benefits are portable.

Source: NHIMA FAQ, https://www.nhima.co.zm/elementor-1783/ (checked 2 November 2026)

**Four rules that cause arguments at the clinic:**

1. **4-month waiting period.** A fully paid member can only access NHIMA services 4 months after registration. There is no waiting period for members aged 65 and above.
2. **6 beneficiaries maximum** — your spouse plus 5 dependants below 18 (NHIMA's current FAQ). *Conflict:* the July 2024 Ministerial Statement to Parliament describes "one plus six", i.e. a principal plus up to six family members, covering seven people. **Give both figures and tell members to confirm with NHIMA (toll-free 8000) before registering dependants.**
3. **Dependants turn 18.** A dependant who turns 18 must register as a principal and contribute separately.
4. **If the principal dies,** registered family members keep benefits for **4 months** following the death.

Sources (checked 2 November 2026): NHIMA FAQ, https://www.nhima.co.zm/elementor-1783/; *National Health Insurance Act No. 2 of 2018*, sections 20 and 22 (https://www.parliament.gov.zm/sites/default/files/documents/acts/The%20National%20Health%20Insurance%20Actl,%20No.2%20of%202018%20Sig.pdf); Ministerial Statement 10 July 2024 (https://www.parliament.gov.zm/sites/default/files/images/publication_docs/Ministerial%20Statement%20%20by%20Mrs%20Masebo%20-%2010.07.24.pdf)

**Scale and the sustainability question (useful for a "is this enough?" lesson):** NHIMA reports **over 5,000,000 registered members** (over 3.5 million principal members and over 1.5 million beneficiaries).

Source: NHIMA Director General's foreword, https://www.nhima.co.zm/foreword-2/ (checked 2 November 2026)

The 2022 NHIMA actuarial report projected that scheme reserves would be exhausted around **2026**, and recommended raising the contribution rate from 2.04% in 2023 towards 3.56% by 2032. That is a projection made in 2022, not a confirmed event, and the recommended rate has **not** been adopted as at 2 November 2026 — the rate remains 2% of basic salary (1% + 1%). Present it as "the scheme's own actuaries flagged a funding gap" rather than as a crisis.

Source: NHIMA Actuarial Report 2022–2032, https://p4h.world/app/uploads/2024/06/2022-Final-NHIMA-Actuarial-Report-2022-2032-1.x73677.pdf (checked 2 November 2026)

**Scale of the scheme (context, useful for a chart):** social health insurance contributions in Zambia rose from **ZMW 1,159.71 million in 2022 to ZMW 2,014.08 million in 2023**, then moderated to **ZMW 1,748.37 million in 2024**.

Source: WHO African Region, *National Health Accounts Estimates for Zambia* (2022–2024), https://www.afro.who.int/sites/default/files/2026-07/Zambia%20NHA%202022-2024.pdf (checked 2 November 2026)

### 1.7 What a Zambian civil servant household actually needs to know

- **NHIMA is already being deducted from your salary at 1% of basic salary** — you do not need a private health policy for the basics; you do need to know the 4-month waiting period, the 6-beneficiary cap, and what is *not* covered.
- **A funeral policy is the single most useful insurance product for a typical civil servant household.** The product is cheap, needs no medical exam, and pays out exactly when a family is least able to pay.
- **Check three things before signing any policy:** (a) the waiting period length, (b) the number of people covered on one policy, (c) the sum assured versus the realistic cost of a funeral.
- **Do not die in the first six months.** This is the single most common reason a funeral policy pays nothing.
- **Keep the claim pack together the day someone dies:** death certificate or burial order, NRC of the deceased, NRC of the claimant, proof of relationship, police report if the death was accidental, the policy document, and a completed claim form. Then file within **6 months** and finish the paperwork within **12 months**.
- **Ask for the Key Fact Statement in writing.** PIA has started issuing these for funeral and life policies.
- **If a claim is refused, escalate to PIA** — they commit to investigating within 10 business days.
- **Do not over-insure with education products you do not need.** Government-school fees were abolished in 2022; education policies are for private, boarding and tertiary fees.

## 2. Scams and fraud

### 2.1 The legal frame: "money circulation scheme" is the Zambian term

Zambia's law has a specific name for Ponzi and pyramid schemes. Under **Section 157 of the Banking and Financial Services Act (BFSA)**, *"a person shall not — conduct, or participate in, a money circulation scheme; or issue a notice, circular, prospectus, proposal or other document inviting the public to subscribe to a money circulation scheme."* Conducting or participating is an offence.

The Bank of Zambia's own definition, used in its public warnings, is the clearest plain-English explanation available:

> *"A money circulation scheme is an unsustainable investment operation that pays returns to its investors from their own money or the money paid by subsequent investors, rather than from profit earned by the individual or organization running the operation. The money circulation scheme usually entices new investors by offering higher returns than other legitimate investments, which are usually in the form of short-term returns. Perpetuation of the high returns requires an ever-increasing flow of money from new investors to keep the scheme going."*

Sources (checked 2 November 2026):
- Bank of Zambia, Microsavers Hub public warning: https://www.boz.zm/Microsavers_Hub_Press_Release.pdf
- Bank of Zambia, joint press statement on Destiny Helpers International depositors (Feb 2023): https://www.boz.zm/DHI_Update_February_2023.pdf

**The investment side is regulated by the Securities and Exchange Commission of Zambia (SEC)**, established through the *Securities Act No. 41 of 2016*. SEC's rule of thumb for members: **"The moment they ask you to recruit others, just know there is a problem."**

Sources (checked 2 November 2026):
- SEC Zambia official site and "Find a Licensee" tool: https://www.seczambia.org.zm/
- SEC Chief Executive Officer Phillip Chitalu, via Zambia Monitor, 20 May 2025: https://www.zambiamonitor.com/securities-and-exchange-commission-steps-up-fight-against-investment-scams/

**On the word "sakawa":** it is a Hausa term ("putting inside") that comes from the West African online-fraud ecosystem and is now used loosely across Zambia for Ponzi and pyramid schemes. The official Zambian legal and regulatory terms are **"money circulation scheme"** (BoZ / BFSA s.157), **"Ponzi scheme"** and **"pyramid scheme"** (SEC). A lesson should teach the official words, because those are the words a member will need when reporting.

Sources (checked 2 November 2026): usage description from https://www.walkerart.org/reader/how-online-love-scams-created-a-culture-the-power-and-influences-behind-sakawa/ and https://www.context.news/digital-rights/sakawa-boys-meet-ghanas-online-romance-scammers (both secondary, describing the term's West African origin); official Zambian usage confirmed via BoZ and SEC material cited above

### 2.3 The scams operating in Zambia now, how to spot each, and where to report

| Scam | What it looks like | How to spot it | Where to report |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Ponzi / pyramid ("money circulation") scheme** | A "money doubling" or "empowerment" offer. New investors' money pays old investors | It asks you to **recruit others**; returns are "guaranteed" and "risk-free"; there is no real product or business; returns are suspiciously regular; the promoter is vague about how money is actually earned | Bank of Zambia (if it is taking deposits), SEC Zambia, CCPC, Police |
| **Fake / phishing job** | "Easy money", "data entry", "online marketing", "no experience needed". Sometimes a WhatsApp or Facebook "HR officer" who asks for a "registration fee", "visa processing" or "exam fee" | **No legitimate employer asks you to pay for a job.** CCPC and the ACC both state this explicitly | ACC (if a public official is involved), Police, CCPC; anti-trafficking contact if you are asked to surrender your passport |
| **Job-linked human trafficking** | Overseas job offer; passport confiscated on arrival; you are made to run online scams | Verify the recruiter and employer **before** you travel or send any money or documents; never hand over your passport to an employer you have not independently verified | Zambia Police Service, Drug Enforcement Commission, Department of Immigration; Anti-Human Trafficking Department under the Ministry of Foreign Affairs |
| **Advance-fee fraud** | A promise of a large sum (a contract, a loan, a prize, a government "fund") with a small fee payable first. Each fee "releases" the next | The fee never ends; the payoff is fictional; the documents are forged | Police, CCPC; SEC if it involves an "investment" |
| **Romance / relationship scam** | A long, carefully built online relationship, ending with a request for money for travel, medical bills, customs, or "visa fees". Very often the first sign is that the person **will not do a live video call** | Never send money to someone you have not met in person; be wary of anyone who refuses a video call but accepts money | Police, CCPC |
| **Investment / crypto scam** | High promised returns, "guaranteed" daily or monthly profits, easy withdrawals at first, then blocked withdrawals | A **licensed** check: SEC's "Find a Licensee" tool, or BoZ/PIA for deposits or insurance. BoZ's own warning: *"be cautious with any investment that promises huge returns without underlying economic activity"* | SEC Zambia (public alerts and licensee register), Bank of Zambia, CCPC, Police |
| **Impersonation of officials ("deepfake")** | A video or article claiming the **Governor of the Bank of Zambia** (or another official) has endorsed an investment platform | BoZ issued a formal public warning about a **deepfake video and fake article** impersonating Governor Dr Denny H. Kalyaly and endorsing an investment platform. The Bank *"neither operates, endorses, nor promotes any investment platform of this nature."* Verify only through official BoZ channels | Report to the Bank of Zambia and Police; do not share or forward the content |
| **Mobile-money fraud** | "Your account has been compromised", a "merchant" requesting a refund, an agent "reversing" a transaction that never happened, someone asking you to approve a payment | Check the **recipient's name** shown on your phone at every step before authorising a transfer. **Never share your PIN — even with family.** Agents themselves face fake reversals, phishing, SIM-swap fraud and USSD-based theft | ZICTA on **\*707#** (all networks), ZICTA call centre **7070**, your mobile money provider, Police |
| **SIM-swap / identity theft** | Your phone suddenly loses signal; then money moves out of your wallet | ZICTA has restricted the number of SIM cards a person can register to **10**. Use **\*101#** to check how many numbers are registered against your National Registration Card. Do not buy pre-registered SIM cards | ZICTA (\*707#, \*101#, 7070), Police |
| **Online shopping / fake online store** | Goods paid for but never supplied; false claims; unfair contract terms | CCPC red flags: **comments/feedback sections turned off**; negative reviews about the seller; unrealistic prices | CCPC on **5678** |
| **Government impersonation** | SMS/WhatsApp claiming to be the government, asking you to "verify" bank details for a fund, grant or empowerment money | **"The Government will never request for personal banking details using an SMS or WhatsApp channel."** Requesting bank details by SMS is prohibited under **Section 308 of the Penal Code** (up to 3 years' imprisonment) | CCPC (5678), ZICTA (7070), Police |

### 2.6 What a Zambian civil servant household actually needs to know

- **Never pay for a job.** No legitimate employer — government or private — asks for a registration fee, an interview fee, a "visa processing" fee or a "security deposit" to give you a job. This is the single most exploited fraud against Zambians in 2026, and it is now formally the ACC's stated position.
- **"They asked me to recruit people" is the tell.** That one sentence distinguishes a Ponzi/pyramid scheme from every legitimate investment, and it is the SEC's own published test.
- **Check three registers before you hand over money:** SEC's "Find a Licensee" (investments), Bank of Zambia's licensed-institutions list (deposits), and PACRA (is the business even registered?).
- **Save the three short codes on your phone:** \*707# to report a scammer, \*101# to check your SIM registrations, and 5678 for consumer complaints. Also 7070 for ZICTA.
- **Never share your PIN, even with family.** And always read the recipient's name shown on your screen before you authorise a transfer.
- **Report fast and report with evidence.** The evidence list is short: screenshots of the chats and the transaction, the phone number, the bank or wallet account the money went to. CCPC will not act without proof of transaction.
- **A word of caution about deepfakes:** the Bank of Zambia has already had its Governor's likeness and voice faked to endorse an investment platform. If an official appears in a video telling you to invest, it is still a scam. Verify through official channels only.

## 3. Running a small business

### 3.1 Business name or company? The single most important structural decision

| | **Business name (sole trader / partnership)** | **Private company limited by shares** |
|---|---|---|
| Governing law | *Registration of Business Names Act No. 16 of 2011* | *Companies Act, 2017* |
| What it is | "a designation which identifies an enterprise"; an association of one or more individuals or corporations in partnership | "a legal entity which is separate from its members (owners). It continues to exist even after the demise or changes of its members" |
| Personal liability | The owner is personally liable for the business's debts | Members' liability is limited to the shares they hold |
| Minimum people | 1 (applicant must be 18+, of sound mind) | At least 2 directors; not less than half of the directors (including the executive director) must be resident in Zambia; a company secretary is required |
| Minimum capital | None stated | **Minimum nominal capital K15,000** |
| Form | BN Form III (in duplicate, signed) | Companies Form 3 (typewritten, signed by directors and shareholders) |
| Time | Name reservation process completed within **24 hours** if forms are correctly completed | Process completed within **24 hours** |
| Annual return | Not applicable | Required — see 3.4 |

Sources (checked 2 November 2026):
- Business Regulatory Review Agency eRegistry, "Business Name" and "Local Company" procedure pages: https://www.businesslicenses.gov.zm/business-procedures/details/business-name and https://www.businesslicenses.gov.zm/business-procedures/details/local-company
- PACRA service information: https://info.pacra.org.zm/what-do-i-do-first/ and https://info.pacra.org.zm/category/business-information/starting-company/page/2/
- *Companies Act, 2017*: https://www.parliament.gov.zm/sites/default/files/documents/acts/Companies%20Act,%202017.pdf

**Plain-English rule for the lesson:** *A business name is you. A company is a separate person.* For a household running a small shop or trading business alongside a civil service salary, a business name is simpler and cheaper. A company is worth it only when you need limited liability or want to bring in investors.

### 3.3 ZRA registration and the tax thresholds that matter

**Step 1 — get a TPIN.** Taxpayers must *"notify the Commissioner General within 30 days of commencement of business"* and apply for a Taxpayer Identification Number (TPIN) and tax-type registration.

Source: ZRA Turnover Tax leaflet, https://www.zra.org.zm/wp-content/uploads/2021/08/Turnover-tax.pdf (checked 2 November 2026)

**Step 2 — find your regime.** The two thresholds a small trader needs are:

| Regime | Threshold | Rate | Effective from |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Turnover Tax (TOT)** — the presumptive turnover tax | Annual turnover **up to K5,000,000** | **0% on turnover of K30,000 or less per annum; 5% on turnover above K30,000 up to K5,000,000** | 1 January 2026 |
| Turnover Tax under the previous rules | Same K5,000,000 ceiling | 0% on the first K12,000 per annum; 5% above | 1 January 2025 |
| **VAT** | Compulsory when taxable supplies exceed **K800,000** in an accounting year; voluntary registration available above K400,000 | 16% standard rate | Threshold unchanged |
| **Income tax** | Turnover **above K5,000,000** | 30% corporate income tax on taxable profit | — |

Sources (checked 2 November 2026):
- *The Income Tax (Amendment) (No. 2) Act No. 17 of 2025*, Ninth Schedule Part II ("Turnover per annum: K30,000 or less — 0 percent; Above K30,000 up to K5,000,000 — 5 percent"), in operation 1 January 2026: https://www.parliament.gov.zm/sites/default/files/documents/acts/Act%20No.%2017%20of%202025,%20The%20Income%20Tax%20(Amendment)(No.%202)%20Act.pdf
- Ceiling raised from K800,000 to K5,000,000 by the *Income Tax (Amendment) Act No. 22 of 2024*, effective 1 January 2025: MMLP legal alert, https://mmlp.co.zm/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/LEGAL-ALERT-SERIES-KEY-CHANGES-TO-TAX-_-LEGISLATION-2025-1.pdf; and PwC Worldwide Tax Summaries, Zambia — Other taxes, https://taxsummaries.pwc.com/zambia/corporate/other-taxes
- ZRA Tax Information page (current thresholds, K5,000,000): https://www.zra.org.zm/tax-information/
- U.S. State Department 2025 Investment Climate Statement (corroborates K800,000 → K5 million and 4% → 5%): https://www.state.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/638719_2025-Zambia-Investment-Climate-Statement.pdf

**⚠️ Source conflict on the exemption figure, and on whether it is monthly or annual:**
- The **Act itself** says "Turnover per annum — K30,000 or less: 0 percent", i.e. an **annual** K30,000 exemption.
- Two secondary sources describe it as a **monthly** cushion of K2,500 (MJ Consultants: "K2,500 monthly or K30,000 annual exemption"; ukhani.com: "0% on your first K2,500 of revenue every month"). A monthly K2,500 exemption would give K60,000 a year, which does **not** match the Act.
- **Use the Act: K30,000 per annum.** The monthly framing is an interpretation error and must not be used in a lesson.

**Turnover tax is charged on GROSS SALES, not on profit.** A turnover-tax taxpayer pays 5% on turnover above the K30,000 annual exemption and **does not deduct expenses** — no stock, transport, rent or wage deductions. This is the single most misunderstood point for a first-time trader, and it is why gross sales must be recorded even when the business is barely breaking even.

Source: ZRA Tax Information and Turnover Tax leaflet, https://www.zra.org.zm/tax-information/ and https://www.zra.org.zm/wp-content/uploads/2021/08/Turnover-tax.pdf (checked 2 November 2026)

**Filling in two thresholds at once is normal.** A business with taxable turnover **above K800,000 but not exceeding K5,000,000** must register for **both turnover tax and VAT**. Above K5,000,000, it moves to income tax plus VAT.

Source: Zambia Taxation Practice Note No. 1 of 2025 (Zambia Institute of Public Accountants, via Scribd), https://www.scribd.com/document/841159247/Taxation-Practice-Notes-No1-2025-v4 (checked 2 November 2026 — secondary but a professional body document)

**Who is excluded from turnover tax:** partnerships (regardless of turnover), consultancy services income, mining operations, income subject to final withholding tax (bank interest for individuals, dividends, Treasury bill/interest income of exempt persons), and anyone voluntarily registered for VAT. Artisanal and small-scale mining threshold remains K800,000 (PwC) / proposed to rise to K5 million.

Sources (checked 2 November 2026): ZRA Turnover Tax leaflet (URL above); PwC Zambia Other Taxes (URL above)

**Rental income tax (relevant to a landlord):** 0% per annum on turnover up to K30,000; 4% per annum between K30,000 and K800,000; 16% per annum above K800,000.

Source: PwC Worldwide Tax Summaries, Zambia — Other taxes, https://taxsummaries.pwc.com/zambia/corporate/other-taxes (checked 2 November 2026)

**Passenger transport (PSV) presumptive tax:** for vehicles below 50 seats, the annual presumptive tax per vehicle has been published as K10,368 (36–49 seats), K7,776 (22–35), K5,184 (18–21), K2,592 (12–17) and K1,296 (below 12, including taxis). **Buses with 50 seats and above were removed from presumptive tax from 1 January 2026** and must now use turnover tax (below K5m) or corporate income tax (above K5m). *The exact per-vehicle amounts for 50-plus-seat buses are marked "Not verified" in the final section.*

Sources (checked 2 November 2026): *Income Tax (Amendment) (No. 2) Act No. 17 of 2025*, section 64A(1A) (URL above); per-vehicle table via https://github.com/openaccountants/openaccountants/blob/main/skills/international/zambia/zm-income-tax.md (secondary, citing PwC); the 50-seat change described in EY Zambia's 2026 National Budget Analysis, https://www.ey.com/content/dam/ey-unified-site/ey-com/en-zm/documents/ey-zm-budget-economic-overview.pdf

### 3.6 What a Zambian civil servant household actually needs to know

- **Register the business name (K166) and get a TPIN.** Do not trade informally for years — ZRA registration and PACRA registration are separate, cheap, and fast (24 hours).
- **Know your tax regime from your annual sales, not your profit.** Under K5 million a year you are on turnover tax: 5% of gross sales above K30,000 a year, with **no expense deductions**. Above K5 million, you are on income tax at 30% of profit.
- **Below K30,000 of sales in a whole year, you owe no turnover tax at all.** That is the "figure below which no tax is due" — and it is **per year, not per month**.
- **If you pass K800,000 of taxable sales in a year, you must also register for VAT** — you can be on turnover tax and VAT at the same time.
- **File on the 14th of every month, even if you sold nothing.** A nil return is still a return.
- **Keep six years of records**, and keep them in English. A mobile-money statement on its own is not enough evidence of turnover if you also take cash.
- **Separate the business money from the household money** — a dedicated merchant wallet (MTN merchant or Airtel Money Pay) with its own records, reconciled monthly against the sales log.
- **If you become a company rather than a business name, remember the annual return to PACRA within 90 days** (K200 for a private company) and the 10-year record retention rule.

### 4.2 The core co-operative rules that protect a member

These are the rules that make a cooperative different from a company or a pyramid scheme. They are also what protects a member's money.

| Rule | What it means | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **One member, one vote — and no proxies** | "Each member or delegate has one vote only at meetings" and "there is no voting by proxy". Your number of shares does **not** buy you extra votes | Act (s.101 in the Chapter 397 numbering; regulation 29 in the 1998/2009 numbering) |
| **No member may hold more than one-fifth of the share capital** | Unless the by-laws provide otherwise for holdings by other co-operative societies. Stops one person controlling a society | Act |
| **The AGM is the supreme authority** | "The authority of the members as joint owners of the business and property of a society shall be vested in the annual general meeting of members, and in any special meeting" | Act, s.128(1) |
| **Members can vote on the surplus** | At the AGM, members "shall confirm or amend the resolution of the board of directors respecting the distribution of the net surplus" | Act, s.128(2) |
| **Every member gets a statement after each AGM** | After each AGM each member (other than a community service society under s.88) "receives a statement or other information showing his share capital or other interest in the society after the apportionment of the annual net surplus for the preceding financial year" | Act, s.128(d) |
| **Your shares are not attachable for your own debts** | "The shares or other interest of a member in the capital of a society shall not be liable to attachment or sale under decree or order of a court in respect of any debt" | Act |
| **You may not be locked out of membership** | "In no case shall a society fix any limit to the number of its members" | Act |
| **No dividend before the accounts are audited** | "A co-operative society shall not declare or pay a dividend or bonus, or distribute any part of its accumulated funds, before the financial statements have been certified by a qualified auditor" | Act (s.129(2) in the 1998 numbering) |
| **Surplus must be paid out within 12 months** | After making provision for reserves, any dividend on share capital, and educational purposes, the society must distribute the surplus within **12 months** of the close of the financial year, as a patronage bonus per the by-laws | Act |
| **The Registrar audits every society at least once a year** | "The Registrar shall audit, or cause to be audited … the accounts of every society at least once in every year" | Act, s.140(1) |
| **A public accountant is required above K200,000 in assets** | Where total assets exceed K200,000 (except a credit union under rule 96), the Registrar may require the annual audit to be done by a person publicly carrying on the profession of accountant in Zambia | Co-operative Societies Rules, rule 107 |
| **First meeting within two months of registration** | Unless the Registrar extends the time, the society must hold a general meeting within two months of receiving its certificate of registration | Act |
| **Notice of meeting: at least 21 days** | Notice of every general meeting must be sent to each member at the address in the register of members, at least 21 days before the meeting; the notice must state the purpose, and the agenda must be made available to each member before the meeting wherever practicable | Act |

### 4.4 Member rights and share capital

**Share capital and membership are two different things.** Equity capital of a co-operative society is "the sum of the shares of the members, the reserve funds and such additional funds or investments as may be provided for in the by-laws". A member's rights arise from **membership**, and a member does not exercise the rights of a member until they have paid the membership fee or acquired the interest specified in the by-laws.

Source: The Co-operative Societies Act (Parliament of Zambia and a-mla.org copies, URLs given in 4.1) — checked 2 November 2026

**Practical member rights a member can point to:**

1. **One vote, in person, no proxies.**
2. **Receive an audited statement.** Section 143(1) of the Act: *"Every society shall, within six months after the end of each financial year, send to every member who requests it, an audited statement of the receipts and expenditures and of the assets and liabilities of the society."* You have to ask — but you can ask, and the answer is legally owed to you.
3. **Inspect the latest balance sheet and audit report.** The 2009 Regulations require the committee to "always keep a copy of the latest annual balance sheet of the registered society together with [the] latest audit report … for inspection during office hours by any member of the registered society."
4. **Receive a statement of your share capital and interest after each AGM** (Act, s.128(d)).
5. **Vote to confirm or amend the board's proposal on how the surplus is distributed** (Act, s.128(2)).
6. **Elect and remove the committee** at a general meeting (2009 Regulations).
7. **Ask for the AGM to be called by the Registrar.** The committee must convene the AGM within **three months** of the end of the financial year once the audit report is received; **where the committee fails to convene the meeting, the Registrar shall convene a special general meeting** which proceeds as if the committee had convened it.
8. **Trigger a Registrar investigation.** The Registrar may, on his own initiative, or on the application of **a majority of the management committee**, **at least one-third of the members of the society**, a supervisory committee, or a creditor who deposits security, conduct **an inquiry into the constitution, administration, management or finances of a registered society, or an audit of the accounts.**

Sources (checked 2 November 2026): Act and Regulations (URLs above); the Registrar's investigative power is applied in the High Court judgment *Zvandasara and Others v Registrar of Co-operative Societies* (case ZWHHC 276/2016), https://zimlii.org/akn/zw/judgment/zwhhc/2016/276/eng@2016-05-11/source (checked 2 November 2026) — in that case the Registrar's power and the AGM agenda under section 54 were central

### 4.5 Interest on shares vs dividends vs patronage bonus — the three different returns

This is the most confused idea in Zambian co-operative finance, and it decides whether a member actually gets value from the society.

| | **Interest on a loan / deposit** | **Dividend on share capital** | **Patronage bonus** |
|---|---|---|---|
| What it is | You have **lent** money to the society (or deposited savings with it). It is a **debt** the society owes you, with a promised rate | A share of the annual **net surplus**, paid to you because you **hold shares** | A share of the annual **net surplus**, paid to you because of the **business you did with the society** |
| What the law says | Interest on loans made by the society to members is restricted in some circumstances (see the dividend condition below) | *"The dividend on share capital shall not exceed **six per centum per annum**"*. A **dividend** is defined as "a member's share of the net surplus divided amongst the members in the same proportion as the paid-up share capital held by them in the society" | Surplus after reserves, dividend and educational purposes is distributed "as payment of patronage bonus in the manner provided for in its by-laws", in proportion to the use made of the society's services |
| Who decides | The society's terms, within the Act | The board proposes; the **AGM confirms or amends** it | The board proposes; the **AGM confirms or amends** it |
| Key condition | A society may not pay a dividend if the rate of interest on loans it grants to members exceeds 10% per annum | **No dividend or bonus may be declared or paid, and no part of accumulated funds distributed, before the financial statements have been certified by a qualified auditor** | Same audit condition |

Sources (checked 2 November 2026): Act (URLs above); FAOLEX 1998 Act, https://faolex.fao.org/docs/texts/zam52208.doc; Co-operative Societies Rules 1964/1972 (older rules) via FAOLEX https://faolex.fao.org/docs/pdf/hk90141.pdf

**⚠️ Conflict on the dividend cap:** the 1998 Act's rule is a maximum of **6% per annum** on share capital. The **older** Co-operative Societies Rules state "No registered society shall pay a dividend on share capital exceeding **five per cent per annum** of the capital actually paid up", and that a society may not pay a dividend at all while the interest rate on loans to members exceeds 10% per annum. Both are cited above. **Use 6% as the current statutory ceiling** (the 1998 Act and the Chapter 397 Act both state six per centum) and note the older 5% rules.

**Two categories that get nothing:**
- A **community service society** registered under section 88 "shall not pay a dividend or patronage bonus, and no part of the net surplus arising from the yearly operations of the society shall be paid to any member or patron" (s.89(1)).
- Where a society's objects fall under sections 40 or 41, its by-laws may provide that **no dividend** is paid on share capital, **no patronage bonus** is paid, and the surplus is transferred to reserve for unforeseen losses or to improve services.

Source: The Co-operative Societies Act (Parliament of Zambia and a-mla.org copies, URLs given in 4.1) — checked 2 November 2026

**The lesson in one sentence:** *A dividend pays you for owning; a patronage bonus pays you for using; and neither can be paid until an auditor has signed the accounts.*

### 4.6 How to read a cooperative's financial statements

**The four documents you should be able to get hold of:**

| Document | What it tells you | Where it comes from |
|---|---|---|
| **Balance sheet** | What the society owns (assets) against what it owes (liabilities) on a specific date — including **share capital**, **reserves** and **members' savings** | Act, s.143(1); must be kept and open to member inspection (2009 Regulations) |
| **Income and expenditure account** (the co-operative's "profit and loss") | Income against expenditure for the financial year — the source of the **net surplus** | 2009 Regulations require it to be sent to the Registrar with the accounts and balance sheet |
| **Auditor's report** | Whether the auditor got all the information and explanations needed, and whether in the auditor's opinion the balance sheet "is properly drawn up so as to exhibit a true and correct view of the state of the society's affairs" | Act; report is made to the **members** |
| **Statement of the member's own interest** | Your share capital and other interest in the society after the apportionment of the annual net surplus | Act, s.128(d) — issued after each AGM |

**A simple member test in four questions:**

1. **Do members' savings and share capital appear in the liabilities?** If savings are not shown, the balance sheet is not showing you where your money is.
2. **Is there a net surplus, and what happened to it?** Under the Act it must go to reserves first, then a dividend (capped at 6%), then education, and the remainder as a patronage bonus — **and it must be distributed within 12 months** of the year end.
3. **Is there an auditor's report, and is it unqualified?** A qualified opinion or a missing audit report is the single biggest warning sign. Above **K200,000** in total assets, the Registrar may require a **public accountant**.
4. **Is there a surplus being held back with no explanation?** Remember: **no dividend or bonus may be declared or paid, and no part of accumulated funds distributed, before the financial statements have been certified by a qualified auditor.**

Sources (checked 2 November 2026): Act (URLs above); 2009 Regulations (URLs above); rule 107 on public accountants, via https://www.icnl.org/research/library/zambia_zambiacoopsocieties/ (checked 2 November 2026)

### 4.7 What a member can do if the committee is not reporting

A practical escalation ladder, all of it in the Act and Regulations:

| Step | Action | Legal basis | Time limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Ask the committee in writing** for the latest annual balance sheet and audit report, and for the date of the last AGM and the date of the next one | 2009 Regulations require the committee to keep these open for inspection by any member during office hours | Committee must convene the AGM within **3 months** of the financial year end once the audit report is received |
| 2 | **Request your audited statement in writing.** Every member is entitled to an audited statement of receipts and expenditures and of assets and liabilities, sent within **6 months** of the financial year end | Act, s.143(1) | 6 months after financial year end |
| 3 | **Call a special general meeting.** Members may requisition a special general meeting; if the committee will not call the AGM, the **Registrar shall convene a special general meeting** which proceeds as if the committee had convened it | 2009 Regulations; Act (powers of the Registrar to call meetings, s.123(3) quorum rule) | — |
| 4 | **Apply to the Registrar for an inquiry or audit.** The Registrar may conduct an inquiry into the constitution, administration, management or finances of the society, or audit the accounts, on his own initiative or on the application of **a majority of the management committee, at least one-third of the members, a supervisory committee, or a secured creditor** | Registrar's investigative power, applied in *Zvandasara v Registrar of Co-operative Societies* | — |
| 5 | **Withdraw your membership and take your share value.** The society's share of a member's interest is protected against attachment for the member's own debts, and the Registrar's register is the record. Note that in some cases withdrawal is subject to the by-laws and to a notice period — check the by-laws before relying on it | Act (register of members, s.100; member's interest not attachable) | Check by-laws |
| 6 | **Report to the Registrar** that the society has not filed its annual returns or audit report. Every society must furnish the Registrar with annual and other returns and information as required, and must file an annual return | Act, s.143(2); 2009 Regulations | Within 6 months after the financial year end |

A lesson should say "there are tens of thousands of registered co-operatives and millions of members" and cite the source for whichever number it uses, rather than presenting either figure as unarguable.

Sources (checked 2 November 2026): all URLs above

### 4.9 What a Zambian civil servant household actually needs to know

- **One member, one vote, no proxies.** If you are on a co-operative committee, you are not there to represent a shareholding; you are there as a member. This is exactly why a co-operative is not a pyramid scheme.
- **You are owed an audited statement if you ask for it** within six months of the financial year end. Keep the request in writing.
- **No dividend without an audit.** If a committee announces a bonus but there is no auditor's report, that is a red flag, not good news.
- **A dividend is capped at 6% a year on share capital; the bigger return in a working co-operative is the patronage bonus**, which depends on how much business you brought in. Ask which one your society is paying.
- **You can call the Registrar.** One-third of the members can require an inquiry or audit. Most members do not know this exists.
- **Read three lines in the accounts:** members' savings and share capital in the liabilities; the net surplus and where it went; and whether the auditor's opinion is clean.
- **The most common real failure is not theft — it is silence.** A committee that does not call an AGM, or calls one without an audited balance sheet, is the signal to act.

### 5.2 Evidence on why savings fail

**Reason 1 — Zambians save, but mostly not where they think they do.**

World Bank Global Findex 2025 microdata for Zambia:

| Saving behaviour | Share of adults |
|---|---|
| Saved using **mobile money** | **65.8%** |
| Saved at a **bank** | **15.4%** |
| Saved at a **SACCO or co-operative** | Not reported in the figures checked |
| Saved formally (any institution) | Not reported in the figures checked |

Source: World Bank Global Findex 2025, Zambia country microdata, https://www.worldbank.org/en/publication/globalfindex/Indicator (checked 2 November 2026)

**Why mobile money dominates: the cash-out fee.** Money that is saved as mobile money is money you can spend from the same wallet. To move it into a bank account you must first withdraw it — which costs an agent fee — and then deposit it, which costs time. This is not a knowledge gap; it is a **design and cost gap**.

**Reason 2 — the emergency plan for most Zambians is the family, not the savings account.**

| Main source of money in an emergency within 30 days | Share of adults |
|---|---|
| **Family, relatives or friends** | **34.9%** |
| From working (wages, business, farming) | 18.5% |
| **Savings** | **11.1%** |
| A loan from a financial institution, employer or private lender | 8.2% |

Source: World Bank Global Findex 2025, Zambia country microdata, https://www.worldbank.org/en/publication/globalfindex/Indicator (checked 2 November 2026)

**Read that again: only about one adult in nine says savings is their main emergency money; more than one in three says it is family or friends.** This is the single most important fact for a savings lesson — and it is the precise problem BISWIC's own savings buckets exist to solve. It is also why an emergency-fund lesson must be taught as a **household agreement** (who do you call first?) rather than as a savings target.

**Reason 3 — the real return on savings was negative for years, so "saving" felt like losing.**

**Reason 4 — savings groups work, but they are badly recorded and badly linked to formal finance.**

**The scale of savings groups, and how little of it is formalised.** The Bank of Zambia's *Zambanker* (March 2025) reports that savings groups in Zambia collectively saved **ZMW 50.5 million**, averaging **ZMW 29,000 per group**; disbursed **ZMW 27.5 million in loans**, averaging **ZMW 16,000 per group**; and set aside **ZMW 2.4 million in social funds** for emergencies and community projects. But **only 13% of savings groups have bank accounts and just 11% use mobile money services.**

**Reason 5 — the "small small" (daily susu) collector takes a cut.** A PhD study of Zambian microfinance records the practice directly: *"the Susu collector, a trustworthy person in the community, visit[s] clients on a regular basis collecting very small deposits over the course of a month. At the end of the month the Susu collector returns the accumulated savings to the client **but keeps a day's savings as a commission or fee**."*

Source: PhD thesis, University of Hertfordshire, "the impact of microcredit on household expenditure and…", https://uhra.herts.ac.uk/id/eprint/15910/1/15062571%20OSEI%20Nana%20Final%20Version%20of%20PhD%20Submission.pdf (checked 2 November 2026 — academic source)

**Reason 6 — savings groups work well for goals and badly for shocks.** The classic study of Zambian chilimba is unusually blunt:

> "**Default is rare, as the commitment is taken very seriously.**… Chilimba groups are evidence that very poor people desire savings… **It is appropriate only for people with some regular source of income. It does not serve as a safety net in emergencies.** Long-term loans are not possible. A limitation is its openness and lack of structure that permit potential abuse."

Source: Caroline O'Reilly, "Urban women's informal savings and credit systems in Zambia", *Development in Practice* Vol 6 No 2 (1996), https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/0961452961000157714 (checked 2 November 2026)

**The same source gives the group mechanics worth teaching:** group sizes of **4 to 20 members**, mostly women's groups; members agree a fixed, regular cash contribution, paid out **in a specified order**; market groups are larger with small daily contributions, smaller groups make larger but less frequent contributions; new members join **at the end of the rotation**.

Source: same article (checked 2 November 2026)

**And the survival-rate claim should not be repeated uncritically.** A review of savings-group literature notes: *"Unconfirmed reports put the survival rate of savings groups at 90% through a few studies confirm the results… **Unsubstantiated claims have been made for a long-term survival rate better than 90 percent, but very few studies or long-term management information system (MIS) results have been able to verify these assertions** (Allen & Panetta, 2010)."*

Source: JHSSS review of informal financial services in Zambia, https://media.neliti.com/media/publications/424103-brief-review-of-informal-financial-servi-a29a5ce7.pdf (checked 2 November 2026)

**And the reason savings groups collapse is usually governance, not arithmetic.** A 2026 study on using savings groups to collect NHIMA contributions in rural Zambia found that participants "identified the temptation for people in leadership to misapply their contributions as high", that "some SGs in other places had been disbanded because of mismanagement of funds", and that groups operate on a "cycle-based saving system" in which members disband after a cycle — which "ultimately results in instability."

Source: "Harnessing rural community savings groups for contribution…", PMC, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13371017/ (checked 2 November 2026)

### 5.3 Target saving — the method the current library does not teach

The current library teaches an **emergency fund** (a buffer for shocks). It does not teach **target saving** (a fund for a known, dated goal). The evidence says both are needed, and the mechanics differ:

| | Emergency fund | Target fund |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | An unexpected bill | A known goal with a date: school fees, a funeral, a laptop, a business deposit |
| Amount | A multiple of monthly essentials | **The cost of the goal divided by the number of paydays left** |
| Where it lives | Where it is instantly reachable | Where it earns the best real return for the time available — a fixed deposit or Treasury-bill fund if there are 3+ months, mobile money if there are days |
| When it is a success | You never touch it | You hit the number on the date |

**Why target saving works better for Zambians than an emergency fund alone:** it converts an abstract sacrifice into a visible countdown. A member who knows "the school fees are K12,000 and there are 6 paydays left" is saving **K2,000 per month**, which is a decision. "Save K100 every month for a rainy day" is a habit, and habits lose to months.

**The arithmetic, in plain English:** goal ÷ paydays remaining = the automatic transfer on payday. If the paydays do not divide evenly, round **up** — the last payday carries the remainder.

### 5.5 Practical saving methods that work for someone paid monthly in cash

These are the methods that survive contact with a real monthly salary. Each is stated with the reason it works.

**1. Save on payday, not on payday minus one.** A civil servant's PAYE and NHIMA are deducted at source, so **net pay lands in one place on one day**. The saving must leave on that day too. Standing instructions to a separate wallet, or a scheduled transfer, remove the decision. The failure mode this fixes: the money arrives and is spent within two days, and the saver concludes "I can't save" when in fact they saved late.

**2. Use a second wallet, not a second bank.** BoZ data shows 65.8% of Zambians save via mobile money and only 15.4% at a bank. A **separate mobile money wallet** — not a new tab, a new wallet, ideally linked to a different phone number so it is not visible on your main screen — is the fastest route from "I intend to save" to "I have saved". The behavioural reason: it is invisible at the point of spending.

**3. Name the goal on the wallet.** A wallet labelled "SCHOOL FEES 2027" is a different psychological object from one labelled "SAVINGS". Target saving works when the amount and the date are attached to a specific want.

**4. Set the transfer on payday by division, not by will.** Goal ÷ paydays remaining. If you have 9 paydays before school fees and the goal is K18,000, the transfer is K2,000 — every payday, automatically, including the months when something has gone wrong. Willpower is unreliable in month 7; a standing instruction is not.

**5. Keep the emergency fund where it is reachable in 24 hours, and accept a lower return for it.** The emergency fund is insurance, and insurance is bought by paying a small certain cost for a large uncertain one. **Do not put the emergency fund in a 91-day Treasury-bill fund** — that defeats the purpose of an emergency fund. This is the one place where the "maximise the return" advice is wrong.

**6. Use the savings group for a goal, not for the emergency fund.** The evidence is unusually clear: chilimba "does not serve as a safety net in emergencies", it "is appropriate only for people with some regular source of income", and default is rare precisely because the commitment is social. So a chilimba is a **good** instrument for school fees and **a bad** one for the hospital bill.

**7. Keep the two funds in two different places.** A savings circle where the money physically sits with a collector is a claim on that collector. The emergency fund should be in the member's own name, in their own account.

**8. Do not borrow to build savings.** (This corrects the current library's "snowball" strategy, which suggests borrowing K2,000 from the cooperative's soft loan to seed an emergency fund. For a cooperative, promoting borrowing to fund saving increases member indebtedness and puts the soft-loan bucket at risk. It also teaches the wrong lesson for a member with a 390% mobile-money loan outstanding.)

**9. Check the inflation line, not just the rate.** At 6.1% inflation, a savings account paying 3% is losing about 3 points of purchasing power a year. The comparison a member should make is: *my return minus inflation*, not *my return*.

**10. Make the first amount small and the first date fixed.** K100 on the 28th of every month, every month, for six months, is K2,400 and a habit. The evidence on why savings fail says the binding constraint is not knowledge but consistency, and consistency is set by the size of the first step.

### 5.6 What a Zambian civil servant household actually needs to know

- **You are not bad at saving; you are saving in the wrong place.** Two thirds of Zambians save via mobile money, and money in the wallet you shop from is money you spend.
- **Your country's savings plan is your family.** 34.9% of Zambians would rely on family or friends for emergency money; only 11.1% say savings. If your household has no agreed plan for who is called first and how much is needed, that is the gap your emergency fund closes.
- **Compare against inflation.** With inflation at 6.1% (September 2026), a savings account at 2–4% is losing money in real terms. The Bank of Zambia says the real savings rate only turned positive in February 2026.
- **Chilimba is a goal tool, not an emergency fund.** Use it for the thing with a date; keep the emergency money in your own account.
- **Write down the schedule**: goal amount, paydays remaining, transfer amount per payday. Round up.
- **A savings circle with a phone ledger is better than one with a notebook** — the record is what turns savings into credit later.
- **Never pay someone else to hold your savings** unless the group is one you trust and the ledger is written. The "susu" collector keeps a day's saving as commission.

### Two corrections that should be made whatever else happens

1. **"Sakawa" is mis-defined in M7 and M13.** Both lessons state that "sakawa" means advance-fee fraud ("send K500 to get K5,000"). In Zambian usage *sakawa* is the popular word for a **Ponzi/pyramid scheme**; the official Zambian legal term is a **"money circulation scheme"** (Banking and Financial Services Act, s.157) and SEC's terms are "Ponzi scheme" and "pyramid scheme". As written, M13 double-counts (sakawa at #1, pyramid at #2) and M13's own module title lists "Sakawa, Kachalo & Pyramid Schemes" as three separate dangers when two of them are the same thing. A member who reports "a sakawa scheme" to the police or the Registrar will be describing the wrong crime.
2. **"Kachalo" and "kacha loan" are undefined labels.** Neither term appears in any Zambian regulator's publications (BoZ, SEC, PIA, CCPC). The lessons should use the official terms — "loan shark", "moneylender", "money circulation scheme" — and keep the local words as flavour, not as the thing you report.

## Candidate new modules

### 1. Insurance and Claims: Covering a Funeral, a Hospital Bill and Your Family's Future

Replace the single lesson in M11 with a three-lesson module. Lesson one: the four products, with the funeral policy first because it is the only cover most of this audience can and should buy (no medical exam, cheap, pays at the worst possible moment), including waiting periods of 3–6 months and the fact that most families buy it too late. Lesson two: NHIMA, corrected — 1% employee plus 1% employer of basic salary, no ceiling, the 4-month waiting period, the 6-beneficiary cap, the exemptions at 65 and above, the 350+ accredited facilities, and what the published benefit package actually includes (including dental and vision, which the current lesson wrongly excludes). Lesson three: **how to claim** — the document list (death certificate or burial order, NRC of the deceased and of the claimant, proof of relationship, police report if the death was accidental, the policy document, a signed claim form or a fingerprint certified by a Commissioner of Oaths), the 6-month filing deadline and 12-month documentation deadline, and the escalation route to PIA with its 10-business-day investigation commitment, plus the ask for PIA's new Key Fact Statement before signing anything. This module matters because it is where the existing lesson is both thinnest and factually wrong, and because funeral cover is the single most cost-effective risk transfer available to a Zambian civil servant household.

### 2. Your Cooperative: Member Rights, AGMs and How to Read the Accounts

Replace M12's promotional single lesson with a member-rights module, keeping the soft-loan and welfare content but moving it after the rights content. What the Act and Regulations actually give a member: one vote, no proxies, a cap of one-fifth on any one member's shareholding, the AGM as the supreme authority, the fixed order of business at the AGM (the directors' report, the Treasurer's report and the financial statement, the auditor's report, the apportionment of the net surplus, elections, appointment of the auditor), the right to receive **an audited statement of receipts and expenditures and assets and liabilities on request within six months of the financial year end**, the right to inspect the latest balance sheet and audit report at the committee's office, the right to vote on how the surplus is distributed, the right to elect and remove the committee, and the right for **one-third of the members** to require the Registrar to inquire into the society's management or audit its accounts. It would also teach the three returns properly — interest on a deposit is a **debt**; a **dividend** on share capital is capped at **6% a year** and cannot be paid before an auditor certifies the accounts; a **patronage bonus** is the bigger return and depends on the business you brought — and how to read four documents: the balance sheet, the income and expenditure account, the auditor's report and your own statement of share interest. This module matters because M12 is currently a sales pitch for the cooperative that teaches a member none of the rights that protect their money in it.

### 3. Running a Small Business: Register, Tax and Keep the Records

A wholly new module, because nothing in the current library touches it. Lesson one: the structural decision — a **business name** (Registration of Business Names Act No. 16 of 2011; K166 to register; the owner is personally liable) versus a **private company** (Companies Act 2017; K1,000 walk-in or K925 online; minimum nominal capital K15,000; at least two directors; separate legal personality; an annual return to PACRA within 90 days costing K200). Lesson two: the tax map — turn over more than **K5,000,000** a year and you are on income tax; below it you are on **turnover tax at 5% of gross sales above K30,000 a year and nothing at all below K30,000**, with the important warning that turnover tax is charged on **gross sales, not profit**, so expense records do not reduce it; and crossing **K800,000** of taxable sales adds **VAT** on top. Lesson three: the calendar and the books — register with ZRA within 30 days of starting, file on the **14th of every month** even in a nil month, keep **six years** of records in English, keep a sales log alongside the bank and mobile-money statements (a mobile-money statement alone does not prove turnover if you also take cash), and use a merchant wallet so that business money is separated from household money. It would also settle the mobile-money cost question honestly: incoming merchant payments carry no levy, so the real costs are the agent withdrawal fee and the collection fee (MTN publishes a 1%–4% band). This module matters because a meaningful share of civil servant households trade, the exemption threshold changed on 1 January 2026, and the current library's only business-adjacent content is a warning about "land scams".

### 4. Where to Report Fraud — and How to Try to Get Your Money Back

A companion to M13 rather than a replacement for it. M13's ten scams and its 60-second detection test are good; what is missing is the second half of the story. This module would teach the reporting map — **\*707#** on ZICTA for a scammer's number or a stolen phone, **\*101#** to check how many SIM cards are registered against your National Registration Card, **7070** to call ZICTA, **5678** for the Competition and Consumer Protection Commission (with the rule that you must contact the trader first and bring proof of transaction), the Police for criminal fraud and identity theft, SEC for anything presented as an investment, the Bank of Zambia for anything taking deposits or impersonating the Bank, and the Registrar for a cooperative. It would include the official numbers so the lesson is usable: **1,124 online scam cases and K38,948,000 lost** between 2023 and mid-2024, of which K12,407,990 was recovered and 117 convictions followed; **43,500 SIM cards deactivated** and K19.8 million secured in the first half of 2026; and **K21 million saved through \*707#** in the first half of 2025. It would also teach the honest recovery path — stop sending, screenshot everything, get the transaction IDs and the receiving number, report within days because the police and ZICTA can act on fresh SIM and wallet data — and it would remove M13's current line that reporting to the police is "not always productive". Finally, it would cover the scams M13 omits: romance scams, fake overseas jobs linked to forced online-scam work (79 arrested in a Lusaka operation in August 2026), deepfake videos of the BoZ Governor endorsing investment platforms, and fake online shops.

### 5. Saving in Real Terms: Inflation, Savings Groups and the Tools on Your Phone

An extension of M8 rather than a replacement. M8 has the right idea and the right shape, but it does not connect saving to the thing that actually determines whether saving works — **inflation**. At 6.1% (September 2026) with the Bank of Zambia reporting that the **real savings rate only turned positive in February 2026**, a savings account paying 2–4% is losing money, and the lesson should say so plainly and then show where the money actually is: the BoZ's quarterly *Interest Rates and Bank Charges* publication, the 6-month deposit rate of 7.4% and the composite Treasury bill yield of 11.5% (June 2026), and the mobile money money-market funds such as **Patumba** (from K1, tenors of 91 days to 2 years, aiming to track the 365-day Treasury bill rate but not guaranteeing a rate). It would also cover the **target-saving** method the library omits entirely — name the goal, divide by the paydays left, set the transfer on payday, round up — and the **savings group** (chilimba), including the group mechanics (4 to 20 members, fixed contributions, a set rotation order, new members joining at the end) and, crucially, the two evidence-based limits: a chilimba "does not serve as a safety net in emergencies" and is only appropriate for people with a regular income, and a "susu" collector keeps a day's saving as commission. It would close with the digitised options — the Chilimba and KwachaKash apps and Access Bank's Tenga Group Savings (minimum ZMW 100, automated share-out) — because the Bank of Zambia's own diagnosis of savings-group failure is "lack or poor record keeping", which is exactly what a phone ledger fixes. Finally, it would remove M8's advice to borrow K2,000 from the cooperative to seed an emergency fund, and replace it with the safer version: start at K100 on payday, keep the emergency fund in a separate wallet, and let the target fund take the risk.

### Recommended top 3

1. **Your Cooperative: Member Rights, AGMs and How to Read the Accounts** — this is the only one of the three that protects money the member has already committed and can see every month, and it is the one where the current library is least fit for purpose: M12 teaches a promotional narrative and none of the Act's protections, while the members' money sits in the very institution they have legal rights over. It also directly protects BISWIC's own standing, because most member disputes are a misunderstanding about rights rather than an actual dispute about money.
2. **Insurance and Claims: Covering a Funeral, a Hospital Bill and Your Family's Future** — the funeral policy is the cheapest meaningful risk transfer available to a Zambian civil servant household, the existing lesson has factual errors about NHIMA (waiting period, dental and vision cover) that a member could be harmed by at the clinic, and no lesson in the library teaches how to claim, which is the step where insurance either pays or does not.
3. **Running a Small Business: Register, Tax and Keep the Records** — the only genuinely absent topic of the five, with a live tax change on 1 January 2026 that has left small traders and their accountants guessing about the K30,000 exemption, and a structural decision (business name or company) that determines whether a member's personal assets are at risk when the business fails.

## Not verified

Each item below was researched and could not be confirmed from a primary source. None of these figures is presented as fact elsewhere in this document except where the text explicitly says "Not verified" or gives both conflicting figures.

1. **A typical monthly premium for a funeral policy.** No Zambian insurer publishes a standard premium schedule. The only figures I could confirm from official or first-party pages are FSG's one-off joining fees (K20 per nuclear family, K20 per single member, K10 per additional member) and Zanaco's sum-assured range (K20,000–K100,000). **Tried:** Zanaco, FSG/Ideal Funeral Home, FNB Family Protection Plan terms PDF, Stanbic Ubuntu, Old Mutual/Best Life, PIA e-Newsletter. **Needed:** a written premium illustration from a named insurer.
2. **PIA's current list of licensed insurers (2026).** I cited a 2024 PIA public notice (23 licensed general insurers, 10 long-term insurers) found via a third-party host, not via pia.org.zm. **Tried:** pia.org.zm licence registers and the Annual Reports index page. **Needed:** the 2026 register downloaded directly from PIA.
3. **NHIMA's dependants cap.** NHIMA's current FAQ states **6 beneficiaries (spouse plus 5 dependants below 18)**; the Ministerial Statement to Parliament of 10 July 2024 describes "one plus six", i.e. a principal plus up to six family members (seven people). Both are given in section 1.6. **Needed:** written confirmation from NHIMA (toll-free 8000) before any lesson states a number.
4. **Whether the turnover tax exemption is annual or monthly.** The *Income Tax (Amendment) (No. 2) Act No. 17 of 2025* says "Turnover per annum — K30,000 or less: 0 percent". Two secondary sources describe a **monthly** K2,500 cushion, which would give K60,000 a year and does not match the Act. I used the Act. **Tried:** the Act PDF, ZRA's Tax Information page, ZRA's Turnover Tax leaflet, PwC and EY Zambia budget analyses. **Needed:** a ZRA ruling or public notice confirming the monthly computation.
5. **Mobile money transaction levy band amounts.** Two secondary sources conflict: one says the levy runs "from K0.32 on transfers under K150 up to K8.00 on transfers between K5,000 and K10,000"; the other says "transfers are free up to K150" and that the levy on a K5,000 send is K7.50. Both agree the levy applies to **person-to-person transfers only**, is paid by the sender, and **does not apply to cash withdrawals or merchant payments**. **Tried:** the *Mobile Money Transaction Levy Act No. 25 of 2024* and Act No. 22 of 2025 as reported by secondary sources; the Gazette itself was not retrievable. **Needed:** the statutory instrument text.
6. **Presumptive tax per vehicle for buses with 50 or more seats.** From 1 January 2026 these were removed from presumptive tax (Act No. 17 of 2025, s.64A(1A)), but I could not read the substituted Ninth Schedule Part I amounts. The older pre-2026 table (K10,368 for 36–49 seats, K7,776 for 22–35, K5,184 for 18–21, K2,592 for 12–17, K1,296 for below 12) is included in section 3.3 but must not be presented as the current position. **Needed:** the current Ninth Schedule Part I.
7. **Bank-by-bank savings-account and time-deposit rates as at 31 March 2026.** I confirmed the Bank of Zambia publication exists, its date and its coverage, and the extracted text was ambiguous enough that I did not reproduce a per-bank table. Section 5.2 therefore uses only the macro anchors (6-month deposit rate 7.4%, composite T-bill yield 11.5%, average lending rate 27.5%, all June 2026). **Needed:** the PDF read directly, bank by bank.
8. **The exact number of registered Zambian cooperatives.** The Registrar's own 2019 figure is 9,498 societies and about 1,578,340 members; the Ministry of SMEs' 2024 policy reports 94,114 societies and 1,869,005 members at end-2022. The tenfold jump is most likely a change of counting basis, but I could not reconcile the two. **Needed:** a Registrar statement of the counting methodology.
9. **PIA's complaints service-level commitments** (3-day acknowledgement, 10 business days to investigate) are cited from PIA's digital platform site rather than from a dated PIA circular. **Tried:** pia.org.zm and the PIA digital platform. **Needed:** the PIA complaints procedure document with a date on it.
10. **Whether the Zambia Police "Cyber Crime Unit" in Lusaka still operates under that name.** The current library tells members to report there. **Tried:** Zambia Police and Ministry of Home Affairs sources reached during this research. **Needed:** a current, official confirmation of the unit's name, location and reporting route.
11. **A typical Zambian funeral cost (K4,000–K20,000).** This range appears in an insurance-sector commentary on a professional network, not in any government or regulator statistic. Section 1.3 flags it as an estimate.
12. **The mobile money collection fee any particular merchant will be charged.** MTN's official API documentation publishes a **1%–4% band** and says the exact percentage is set commercially and is negotiable. The rate for any named trader is not published. Section 3.5 states the band only.
13. **The "5%" soft-loan rate and the "K1,500 funeral / K2,000 medical" welfare figures used in the current library** are internal BISWIC parameters. I could not verify them from any public source and did not attempt to; they should be confirmed against the cooperative's own rules and the Revised National Cooperative Development Policy 2024 before being republished.
14. **The description of "sakawa" as advance-fee fraud** in the current library (M7 and M13) does not match Zambian usage, where *sakawa* denotes a Ponzi/pyramid scheme. The official Zambian legal term is **"money circulation scheme"** (Banking and Financial Services Act, s.157). I could not find any Zambian official publication using *sakawa* as an advance-fee term; the term's West African origin is documented in the sources cited in section 2.1. This is flagged as a **correction**, not a verification failure, but the exact local usage should be checked with Zambian members before rewriting.
